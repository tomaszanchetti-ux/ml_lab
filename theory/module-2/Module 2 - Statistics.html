Module 2 — Statistics

Revolut's topic 3, verbatim: "Bias in statistics, statistical significance, A/B testing." Probability (Module 1) went from a known world to data: given p, what will I see? Statistics goes the other way: given what I saw, what can I say about the world — and how sure am I? Same format: idea, formula, Revolut case, 30-second answer, exercises. Answers at the back.

The one idea of this module

You never see the whole population — you see a sample. Every number you compute from a sample is an estimate with an error. Statistics is the discipline of saying how big that error is, and of not fooling yourself.

Two kinds of error, and they never go away: noise (random — shrinks with more data) and bias (systematic — more data does not help). Confidence intervals, p-values and sample sizes deal with noise. Good experiment design deals with bias. An interviewer wants to hear that you know which is which.

B1Population, sample, estimator

WordMeaningRevolut example
Populationeverything you care aboutall Revolut customers; all future transactions
Parametera true, fixed, unknown number about the population (μ, σ, p)the true fraud rate; the true conversion rate of a new screen
Samplethe part you actually observedthe 20,000 users in the experiment; last month's transactions
Statistic / estimatea number computed from the sample (x̄, s, p̂)the fraud rate you measured last month
Estimatorthe recipe that turns a sample into an estimate"count frauds, divide by transactions"

Notation: Greek or plain letters for the truth (μ, p), hats and bars for estimates (x̄, p̂). The sample mean x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ estimates μ; the sample proportion p̂ = k/n estimates p; the sample standard deviation s estimates σ (it divides by n − 1 instead of n — a small correction that removes a bias; one line is all you need to say).

Law of large numbers: as n grows, the sample mean converges to the true mean. That's the coin from Module 1 settling on 0.5. It tells you estimates get good; the next sections tell you how good at a given n.

Maximum likelihood (one paragraph, in case it's asked): the standard recipe for building an estimator — choose the parameter value under which the data you observed would have been most likely. For a coin with 7 heads in 10, the MLE of p is 0.7; for a normal, the MLE of μ is the sample mean. Training a logistic regression is maximum likelihood: find the weights that make the observed outcomes most probable.

30 seconds — "population vs. sample, parameter vs. statistic""The population is everything I care about — all customers, all future transactions — and a parameter is a true number about it, like the real fraud rate, which I never see. A sample is what I observed, and a statistic is what I compute from it, like last month's measured fraud rate. The statistic is an estimate of the parameter and it comes with an error; the job of statistics is to quantify that error and to make sure the sample isn't systematically different from the population."

B2Bias — the error that more data doesn't fix

"Bias in statistics" is the first item on their list. The word has two related meanings; know both.

Meaning 1 — bias of an estimator

bias = E[estimate] − truth variance = how much the estimate jumps around from sample to sample

Imagine repeating the study many times and plotting every estimate as a dart. Bias is how far the centre of the darts is from the bullseye; variance is how scattered they are. An estimator is unbiased if on average it hits the truth.

Low varianceHigh variance
Low biastight, on target — the goalcentred on target, but scattered → get more data
High biastight, in the wrong place — the dangerous one: looks precisescattered and off

Total error: mean squared error = bias² + variance. More data shrinks variance. Nothing about sample size shrinks bias. Sometimes a slightly biased estimator with much lower variance is the better choice — that trade is the heart of Module 3 (regularisation is exactly this).

Meaning 2 — biased data: the sample doesn't represent the population

BiasWhat happensRevolut example
Selection biaswho gets into the sample depends on the thing you're measuringSurveying satisfaction only among users who opened the app this week. Testing a feature only on users who opted in.
Survivorship biasyou only see the ones that made it through a filterA credit model trained only on approved loans never learns what the rejected applicants would have done. Analysing "customers with 3+ years" to understand churn.
Label / observation biasthe outcome is only recorded for some casesFraud is confirmed only for transactions someone investigated — the model learns the investigators' habits.
Non-response biasthose who answer differ from those who don'tNPS surveys: angry and delighted customers reply; the middle doesn't.
Measurement biasthe instrument is systematically offAn event that isn't logged on old Android versions.
Look-ahead bias / leakageusing information that wasn't available at decision timeA default model that uses "days past due" recorded after the default.
Confirmation biasyou look until you find what you expected, then stopSlicing an A/B test by 30 segments and reporting the one that "worked".

Two classic traps that look like bias

Simpson's paradox — the mix changes the answer A new dispute flow resolves more cases than the old one in every segment, yet less overall:
Small disputesLarge disputesOverall
Old flow800 / 1,000 = 80 %100 / 500 = 20 %900 / 1,500 = 60 %
New flow170 / 200 = 85 %325 / 1,300 = 25 %495 / 1,500 = 33 %
The new flow was given mostly the hard cases. The overall rate compares different mixes, not different flows. Fix: compare within segments, or — better — randomise who gets which flow. This is the strongest single argument for A/B testing.
Regression to the mean — extremes drift back on their own Pick the 50 merchants with the most disputes last month and send them a warning. Next month their disputes fall. Did the warning work? Not necessarily: an extreme month is partly bad luck, and luck doesn't repeat. The same merchants would have improved with no email. Same reason a very tall father tends to have a son who is tall, but less so. Fix: a control group selected the same way.
30 seconds — "what is bias in statistics?""Two things. For an estimator, bias is systematic error: if I repeated the study many times, the average of my estimates would still miss the truth — as opposed to variance, which is random scatter that more data reduces. And for data, bias means my sample isn't representative: selection bias, survivorship bias — like training a credit model only on approved loans, so it never sees how rejected applicants behave. The key point is that more data fixes variance but never bias; bias is fixed by design — randomisation, control groups, careful definition of who's in the sample."

B3How wrong is my estimate? Standard error and the Central Limit Theorem

Take a sample of n, compute the mean. Take another sample: a slightly different mean. The spread of those possible means is the standard error:

SE of a mean = σ / √n SE of a proportion = √( p(1 − p) / n )
  • Standard deviation (σ) describes how spread out individuals are. Standard error describes how uncertain your estimate is. People mix them up constantly; don't.
  • The √n rule: to halve the error you need four times the data. Going from 1,000 to 4,000 users buys as much as 10,000 to 40,000. Precision gets expensive.

The Central Limit Theorem

Whatever the shape of the raw data — skewed, heavy-tailed, yes/no — the mean of a large enough sample is approximately normal, centred on the true mean, with standard deviation σ/√n. That is why the normal distribution is everywhere in statistics, and why confidence intervals and tests work on data that isn't remotely bell-shaped.

  • "Large enough": ~30 for mild shapes; hundreds or thousands for heavy tails like revenue — a few whales can dominate the mean of even a big sample.
  • It's about means and sums, not about the raw data. The data doesn't become normal; the average does.
RevolutTransaction amounts: σ ≈ €600 (heavy tail). Average of 100 transactions: SE = 600/√100 = €60. Average of 10,000: SE = €6. Average of 1,000,000: SE = €0.60. A daily "average transaction value" on a small market can move by tens of euros with nothing having happened.
30 seconds — "what is the Central Limit Theorem and why does it matter?""If I take a large sample and compute its mean, that mean is approximately normally distributed around the true mean, with spread sigma over root n — whatever the shape of the underlying data. It matters because it lets me put error bars on an average and run tests even when the data is skewed, like transaction amounts. Two caveats: it's about the mean, not the data, and with very heavy tails 'large enough' can mean thousands, not thirty."

B4Confidence intervals — the estimate with its error bars

95 % CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE (90 %: ± 1.645 × SE 99 %: ± 2.58 × SE)

1.96 comes from the normal curve: 95 % of the area lies within ±1.96 standard deviations (the "±2σ" of Module 1).

WorkedAuto-resolution rate measured at 30 % on 400 disputes. SE = √(0.3 × 0.7 / 400) = 0.0229. CI = 30 % ± 1.96 × 2.29 = 30 % ± 4.5 pp → [25.5 %, 34.5 %]. On 4,000 disputes: ± 1.4 pp. On 40,000: ± 0.45 pp.

What it means — and what it doesn't

  • Correct: "If we repeated this study many times, 95 % of the intervals built this way would contain the true value." The 95 % describes the procedure.
  • Loose but acceptable in business: "we're fairly confident the true rate is between 25.5 % and 34.5 %."
  • Wrong: "95 % of customers fall in this range" (that's about individuals, not the mean). Also wrong: thinking it covers bias — a CI only measures noise. A biased sample gives a tight interval around the wrong number.

Three things make it narrower: more data (√n), less variable data (smaller σ), or accepting less confidence (90 % instead of 95 %).

The link to testing: if the 95 % CI for a difference between A and B does not include zero, the difference is statistically significant at the 5 % level. Same maths, and the CI tells you more — it shows the size.

30 seconds — "what is a confidence interval?""It's a range around an estimate that reflects sampling noise: the estimate plus or minus about two standard errors for 95 %. Strictly, if I repeated the experiment many times, 95 % of such intervals would contain the true value. Practically: we measured 30 % on 400 cases, so the truth is plausibly between 25 and 35, and if someone asks whether it's 30 or 32, the honest answer is we can't tell yet. It only covers random error — if the sample is biased, the interval is confidently wrong."

B5Hypothesis tests and statistical significance

The logic, in four steps

  1. Null hypothesis H₀: "nothing is going on" — no difference between A and B. Alternative H₁: there is a difference.
  2. Compute a test statistic: how many standard errors the observed difference is from zero. z = difference / SE.
  3. p-value: if H₀ were true, how often would chance alone give a result at least this extreme?
  4. If p is below a threshold chosen in advance (α, usually 0.05), reject H₀ and call the result statistically significant.

It's proof by contradiction with probabilities: assume no effect; if the data would be very surprising under that assumption, abandon the assumption.

What a p-value is and isn't

  • Is: P(data this extreme | no real effect).
  • Is not: the probability that H₀ is true. Not the probability the result is a fluke. (That's P(no effect | data) — the flipped conditional. Module 1: you'd need a prior.)
  • Is not: the size or importance of the effect. With a million users, a 0.05 pp lift is "significant" and irrelevant. With 200 users, a 5 pp lift may be "not significant" and real. Statistical significance ≠ practical significance. Always report the effect size and its confidence interval.

The two errors

Truth: no effectTruth: real effect
You conclude "effect"Type I error — false positive. Probability = α (5 %).Correct. Probability = power = 1 − β (aim for 80 %).
You conclude "no effect"Correct.Type II error — false negative. Probability = β.

It is the confusion matrix of Module 1: a test is a detector, α is its false-alarm rate, power is its recall. Power rises with a bigger true effect, a bigger sample, less noisy data, or a looser α. An under-powered test that finds "no significant difference" has shown nothing — absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.

A test by hand: two proportions

Worked — the one to be able to do on a whiteboard A: 1,000 conversions out of 10,000 (10.0 %). B: 1,100 out of 10,000 (11.0 %). Difference = 1.0 pp.
Pooled rate p = 2,100 / 20,000 = 0.105.
SE = √( p(1 − p) × (1/n_A + 1/n_B) ) = √(0.105 × 0.895 × 0.0002) = 0.00434
z = 0.010 / 0.00434 = 2.31 → beyond 1.96 → p ≈ 0.021, significant at 5 %.
95 % CI for the lift: 1.0 pp ± 1.96 × 0.434 pp = [0.15 pp, 1.85 pp]. Say that: "B is better by somewhere between 0.15 and 1.85 points."

Which test — one line each

  • z-test for proportions: comparing two rates (conversion, default, resolution). Most A/B tests.
  • t-test: comparing two means (average spend) when σ is estimated from the data. For large n it is the z-test.
  • Chi-square test: counts in categories — more than two variants, or "is the split across categories what we expected?" Also used to check the 50/50 assignment.
  • Mann–Whitney / bootstrap: when the metric is heavily skewed (revenue) and means are unreliable.
  • One-sided vs. two-sided: two-sided asks "different?", one-sided asks "better?". Default to two-sided; choosing one-sided after seeing the data is cheating.

Multiple comparisons

Test 20 metrics at α = 5 % with no real effect anywhere, and you expect one "significant" result by luck: P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64 %. Remedies: one primary metric decided beforehand; for many tests, a stricter threshold (Bonferroni: α divided by the number of tests).

30 seconds — "what is a p-value? what is statistical significance?""The p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as mine if there were truly no effect. If it's below a threshold I fixed in advance, usually 5 %, I call the result statistically significant: chance alone is an unlikely explanation. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it says nothing about the size of the effect — a tiny lift on a huge sample is significant and useless — so I always report the effect size with its confidence interval. And 5 % means that one in twenty tests of a null effect comes out 'significant' anyway, which is why I pre-register a primary metric."

B6A/B testing, end to end

An A/B test is a randomised controlled experiment: split users at random, change one thing for one group, compare. Randomisation is the point — it makes the two groups identical on average in everything, known and unknown, so any difference is caused by the change. It is the cure for every bias in B2.

The seven steps

#StepWhat you decide
1Hypothesis"Showing the fee before confirmation will raise completed transfers." One change, one expected direction.
2MetricsOne primary metric (the decision metric) + a few guardrails (things that must not get worse: complaints, fraud, latency, revenue).
3Unit of randomisationUsually the customer, not the session or the transaction — otherwise the same person sees both versions and observations aren't independent.
4Sample sizeFrom the baseline rate, the minimum detectable effect (MDE: the smallest lift worth having), α = 5 % and power = 80 %.
5DurationEnough days to reach the sample size, in whole weeks (weekday/weekend, payday effects). Fixed in advance.
6Run — and don't peekCheck health (crashes, sample ratio), not significance.
7Analyse and decideEffect size, CI, p-value on the primary metric; guardrails; then ship / iterate / kill.

Sample size — the formula worth memorising

n per group ≈ 16 × σ² / δ² for a rate: σ² = p(1 − p), δ = the lift you want to detect
WorkedBaseline conversion 10 %, you care about +1 pp. σ² = 0.1 × 0.9 = 0.09; δ = 0.01. n ≈ 16 × 0.09 / 0.0001 = 14,400 per group. Want to detect +0.5 pp instead? δ halves → n × 4 = 57,600 per group. Halving the effect you want to see quadruples the cost. That's the √n rule again, and it's why "let's test it on 300 users" can't work for small effects.

The pitfalls — each is a likely interview question

PitfallWhat goes wrongWhat to do
PeekingChecking daily and stopping the first time p < 0.05. With enough looks, even an A/A test crosses the line: false-positive rate climbs from 5 % to 20–30 %.Fix n in advance; or use sequential-testing methods built for peeking.
Under-powered testToo few users; "no significant difference" means nothing.Compute sample size first. Report the CI: a wide one says "we don't know".
Multiple metrics / segmentsSlice until something is significant.One primary metric; correct for multiple tests; treat segment findings as hypotheses for the next test.
Novelty and primacy effectsUsers click new things because they're new (or resist them at first). The early effect isn't the long-run effect.Run longer; look at the trend by day; compare new vs. existing users.
Sample ratio mismatch (SRM)You asked for 50/50 and got 50.9/49.1 on a million users. Something in assignment or logging is broken, and the result is not trustworthy.Chi-square test on the split before reading any result.
InterferenceOne user's treatment affects another's outcome (referrals, P2P payments, marketplaces, shared merchants).Randomise by cluster (group, region, time slot).
Skewed metricsRevenue per user: one whale lands in B and "B wins".Cap/winsorise outliers, log-transform, use a rank test or bootstrap; more users.
Opt-in featuresComparing users who chose the feature with those who didn't is selection bias.Randomise who is offered it and compare the offered group with the control (intent-to-treat).
Overlapping experimentsTwo tests on the same screen interact.Mutually exclusive groups, or a full factorial design.
  • A/A test: both groups get the same thing. It should show no difference — if it does, your randomisation or logging is broken. It also shows the natural noise of the metric.
  • How to check randomisation worked: compare the groups on things measured before the test (country mix, tenure, past activity) and check the sample ratio.
  • When you can't A/B test (legal, a pricing change for everyone, tiny populations): a holdout region, a before/after with a comparison group (difference-in-differences), or — for automations — shadow mode: the system decides, humans still act, you compare.
30 seconds — "how would you run an A/B test?""I start from one hypothesis and one primary metric, plus guardrails that mustn't get worse. I randomise by customer so the groups are comparable on everything. Before launching I compute the sample size from the baseline, the smallest lift worth detecting, 5 % significance and 80 % power — roughly sixteen times the variance over the effect squared per group — and fix the duration in whole weeks. While it runs I check health and the sample ratio but I don't peek at significance. At the end I report the effect size with its confidence interval, check the guardrails, and decide. The usual ways to get it wrong are stopping early, slicing by many segments, and novelty effects."

B7One-page cheat sheet

  • Parameter / statisticThe truth about the population (never seen) / the number from your sample (an estimate with error).
  • Bias vs. varianceSystematic miss vs. random scatter. MSE = bias² + variance. More data fixes variance, never bias.
  • Biased dataSelection · survivorship (approved loans only) · label bias · non-response · leakage · confirmation. Cure: randomise, control group.
  • Simpson / regression to the meanThe mix can reverse a comparison; extremes drift back on their own. Cure for both: a randomised control.
  • Standard errorσ/√n for a mean; √(p(1−p)/n) for a rate. 4× the data → half the error.
  • CLTMeans of large samples are normal, whatever the data. Heavy tails need much larger n.
  • 95 % CIestimate ± 1.96 SE. Covers noise only, not bias. CI of a difference excluding 0 = significant.
  • p-valueP(data this extreme | no effect). Not P(no effect). Not effect size.
  • Type I / IIFalse positive (α = 5 %) / false negative (β). Power = 1 − β = 80 % target.
  • z-test, two ratesz = (p̂_B − p̂_A) / √(p(1−p)(1/n_A + 1/n_B)); |z| > 1.96 → significant.
  • Sample sizen per group ≈ 16 σ²/δ². Half the effect → four times the users.
  • A/B hygieneOne primary metric · randomise by customer · fixed n and whole weeks · no peeking · check SRM · guardrails · effect size with CI.
