Probability — rules, random variables, distributions

Three sections: A1 the rules (recap — you own these), A2 random variables, A3 the five distributions. About two hours including exercises.

A1The rules of probability (you own these — a one-page recap)

  • Probability = long-run fraction of times something happens. Between 0 and 1. All outcomes sum to 1.
  • AND multiplies: P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B | A). If A and B are independent, P(B | A) = P(B) and it's just P(A) · P(B). "And" always shrinks.
  • OR adds: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). Subtract the overlap so it isn't counted twice.
  • Conditional: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). "B is now the whole world; what fraction of it is A?"
  • Bayes: P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B). Flips the condition. Prior × likelihood ÷ evidence = posterior.
  • Complement: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Often the easiest route: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none).
  • A feature is useful when P(target | feature) ≠ P(target). That inequality is what a model learns.
30 seconds — "explain Bayes' theorem""It lets you flip a conditional probability. A fraud model that catches 90 % of fraud gives you P(flag | fraud); the business needs P(fraud | flag). Bayes multiplies the 90 % by the base rate and divides by how often the model fires overall. With a 0.2 % base rate and a 2 % false-alarm rate, P(fraud | flag) is about 8 % — most alerts are false, because the rare event is swamped by the small error on the huge legitimate population."
Exercises A1
  1. Two independent checks each fail 5 % of the time. Probability that at least one fails?
  2. P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.5, P(A and B) = 0.1. Are A and B independent? What is P(A | B)?
  3. 1 % of customers are fraudsters. A model flags 80 % of fraudsters and 5 % of honest customers. A customer is flagged — probability they're a fraudster? Do the 10,000-customer table first.

A2Random variables — a number that depends on chance

A random variable is a quantity whose value is decided by a random process: the amount of the next transaction, whether a loan defaults (1 or 0), the number of logins tomorrow. Capital letters for the variable (X), small for a value it takes (x).

Discrete vs. continuous

DiscreteContinuous
Takesseparate values: 0, 1, 2, …any value in a range: 12.37 €

Both have aCDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x) — the running total. Percentiles come from it: the median is where F = 0.5.

Expected value — the long-run average

discrete: E[X] = Σ x · P(X = x) continuous: E[X] = ∫ x · f(x) dx

"Each value, weighted by how likely it is." It is a weighted average, not the most likely value and not the middle value.

RevolutA €1,000 loan: 96 % chance of full repayment (gain €80 interest), 4 % chance of default (lose €600). E[profit] = 0.96 · 80 + 0.04 · (−600) = 76.8 − 24 = €52.8. Positive → the loan is worth making at that price. Pricing per customer = choosing the interest so that E[profit] is positive for that customer's P(default). That was your lender model.

Two rules of expectation you should know

  • Linearity: E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], always — even if X and Y are dependent. E[aX] = a·E[X]. So the expected loss of a portfolio = sum of the expected losses of the loans. No independence needed.
  • Products: E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y] only if independent.

Variance and standard deviation — how spread out

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ² σ = √Var(X)

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean (squared so that + and − don't cancel). σ brings it back to the original units, so quote σ. Rules: Var(aX) = a² Var(X); Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) only if independent.

RevolutTwo portfolios with the same expected loss of €24 per loan. Portfolio A: every loan loses about €24. Portfolio B: most lose nothing, a few lose €5,000. Same mean, very different variance. Capital requirements, stress tests and "how bad can a bad month be" are all about variance, not the mean.

Standardising: the z-score

z = (x − μ) / σ "how many standard deviations from the mean"

A z of 3 is "unusual" for most well-behaved variables; for a normal variable, only 0.3 % of values are beyond ±3. This is the simplest anomaly detector: a customer whose spend today is 8σ above their own mean.

Covariance and correlation — do two variables move together

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ)] corr(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y) / (σₓ σᵧ) ∈ [−1, +1]

Correlation is covariance scaled to be unit-free. +1 = perfect straight line up; 0 = no linear relation (there can still be a curved one); −1 = perfect line down. Correlation is not causation: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer.

30 seconds — "what's a random variable, and what are expectation and variance?""A random variable is a number whose value depends on chance — a transaction amount, whether a loan defaults. Its expectation is its long-run average, each value weighted by its probability; its variance is the average squared distance from that mean, and the standard deviation is the square root, in the original units. Expectation tells you what to expect on average; variance tells you how wrong 'on average' can be. In credit, expected loss sets the price; variance sets the capital."
Exercises A2
  1. A dispute costs €0 with probability 0.7, €20 with probability 0.25, €500 with probability 0.05. E[cost]? Which single value is most likely? Is E[cost] a value that ever actually occurs?
  2. X has mean 10 and σ 2. What are the mean and σ of 3X? Of X + 5?
  3. Two independent loans, each with variance of loss 100. Variance of the total loss? What if the two loans were to the same customer (perfectly dependent)?
  4. A customer's daily spend: mean €40, σ €15. Today: €130. z-score? Would you call that unusual?
  5. Correlation between two features is 0. Can they still be related? Give an example.

A3The distributions — the five you must know cold

For each: what it models, its parameter(s), mean and variance, the shape, one Revolut example, and when not to use it. The interviewer will ask "what is X and when would you use it" — that's the whole question.

1 · Bernoulli — one yes/no trial

  • Models: a single event that either happens (1) or doesn't (0), with probability p.
  • Parameter: p. Mean: p. Variance: p(1 − p) — largest at p = 0.5, tiny when p is near 0 or 1.
  • PMF: P(X = 1) = p, P(X = 0) = 1 − p.
  • Revolut: does this transaction turn out to be fraud? does this customer default? The target of every binary classifier is a Bernoulli variable; a model's output ŷ is its estimate of p for that customer.

2 · Binomial — count of successes in n independent trials

  • Models: the number of "yes" in n independent Bernoulli trials with the same p.
  • Parameters: n, p. Mean: np. Variance: np(1 − p).
  • PMF: P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pᵏ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, where C(n, k) = n! / (k!(n − k)!) counts the ways to choose which k trials succeed.
  • Revolut: of 1,000 loans with p = 4 %, how many default? Mean 40, σ = √(1000·0.04·0.96) ≈ 6.2. So 28–52 defaults is "normal"; 70 is not — something changed. Also: an A/B test's conversions per arm are binomial, which is where the A/B formulas come from.
  • Conditions: fixed n, same p every trial, independence. If loans are correlated (same employer goes bankrupt), binomial understates the variance — a classic risk-model error.
Worked5 transactions, each 10 % chance of being disputed. P(exactly 2 disputed) = C(5,2) · 0.1² · 0.9³ = 10 · 0.01 · 0.729 = 0.073. P(at least one) = 1 − P(none) = 1 − 0.9⁵ = 1 − 0.590 = 0.41.

3 · Poisson — count of events in a window, when events are rare and independent

  • Models: how many events occur in a fixed interval of time or space, when they happen independently at a constant average rate λ.
  • Parameter: λ (the average count). Mean: λ. Variance: λ — the same number. That equality is the fingerprint.
  • PMF: P(X = k) = e^(−λ) · λᵏ / k!
  • Revolut: disputes per merchant per month; support tickets per hour; fraud attempts per day on a card. Capacity planning ("how many agents at 3 a.m.?") is Poisson.
  • Relation to binomial: binomial with large n and small p ≈ Poisson with λ = np. That's why rare events (fraud among millions) are Poisson-shaped.
  • Diagnostic: if the observed variance is much larger than the mean (over-dispersion), events aren't independent — they cluster: a fraud ring, a broken merchant, a bug. If variance ≪ mean, something is smoothing them.
WorkedA merchant averages 2 disputes a month. P(0 this month) = e^(−2) = 0.135. P(≥ 5) — small: P(5) = e^(−2)·2⁵/120 = 0.036, and the tail beyond adds ~0.017, so ≈ 0.05. Seeing 8 in a month is a 1-in-500 event → investigate.

4 · Normal (Gaussian) — the bell curve

  • Models: quantities that are the sum or average of many small independent effects. Symmetric, bell-shaped, thin tails.
  • Parameters: μ (centre), σ (width). Mean: μ. Variance: σ².
  • PDF: f(x) = (1 / (σ√(2π))) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²)) — you don't need to compute it; you need the rule below.
  • The 68 / 95 / 99.7 rule: 68 % of values within ±1σ, 95 % within ±2σ (precisely 1.96σ), 99.7 % within ±3σ.
  • Standard normal: μ = 0, σ = 1. Any normal becomes standard via the z-score. That's why tables and tests work with z.
  • Why it's everywhere: the Central Limit Theorem (Part B): averages of anything become normal as the sample grows. So sample means, test statistics and many model errors are normal even when the raw data isn't.
  • Revolut: the average transaction size of a large sample of customers; the error of a well-behaved regression; a test statistic in an A/B test. Not individual transaction amounts, balances, incomes — those are heavy-tailed.
  • How to check normality: histogram; Q-Q plot (points on the diagonal = normal); mean ≈ median; skewness ≈ 0. Formal tests (Shapiro–Wilk) exist but reject everything at large n — use the plots.
WorkedAverage session length is normal with μ = 6 min, σ = 2. P(session > 10 min)? z = (10 − 6)/2 = 2 → beyond +2σ is 2.5 % (the 95 % rule leaves 5 % in both tails, 2.5 % each). P(4 < X < 8) = within ±1σ = 68 %.

5 · Heavy-tailed / log-normal — money

  • Models: positive quantities where most values are small and a few are enormous: transaction amounts, balances, income, LTV, city sizes.
  • Log-normal: X is log-normal if log(X) is normal. Right-skewed; mean > median; the mean is dragged up by the tail.
  • What to do: quote the median and percentiles, not the mean; take log(x) before modelling or before a t-test; be suspicious of any "average" of money.
  • Revolut: "average balance €2,300, median €410" — a few whales. An A/B test on revenue per user is dominated by whales: use a log transform, trimmed means, or a test on the median.

Bonus, one line each

  • Uniform: every value in [a, b] equally likely. Mean (a+b)/2. Random number generators; "no information" priors.
  • Exponential: time between Poisson events; memoryless. Mean 1/λ. Time until the next login, next failure.
  • Geometric: number of trials until the first success. Expected number of tries until a customer converts.

30 seconds — "Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, Normal — when do you use each?""Bernoulli is one yes/no event with probability p — one transaction being fraud. Binomial counts the yeses in n independent trials — defaults in a portfolio of a thousand loans, mean np. Poisson counts rare, independent events in a time window — disputes per merchant per month, with mean equal to variance, so if the variance is much bigger, events are clustering and I'd look for a ring. Normal is the bell curve: sums and averages of many small effects, so sample means and test statistics are normal by the central limit theorem — but individual amounts of money are not; those are heavy-tailed, and I'd log them or use medians."
Exercises A3
  1. A card has a 0.3 % chance of a fraud attempt on any given day, days independent. Which distribution for "fraud attempts in a year"? Mean? Which simpler distribution approximates it, and why?
  2. 10 loans, each 5 % default, independent. P(no defaults)? P(at least 2)?
  3. Support tickets arrive at 12 per hour. P(exactly 0 in a 5-minute window)? (Hint: λ for 5 minutes.)
  4. Model residuals are normal with σ = €8. What fraction of predictions are off by more than €16?
  5. You're told "average transaction is €85, standard deviation €600". What does the σ > mean tell you about the shape? Which statistic would you ask for?
  6. Disputes per merchant per month: mean 3, variance 30. Poisson or not? What might be going on?
  7. Why is the variance of a Bernoulli largest at p = 0.5? What does that mean for predicting a 50/50 event vs. a 1 % event?

AAnswers — check only after you've written yours

A1

  1. P(at least one fails) = 1 − P(both pass) = 1 − 0.95² = 1 − 0.9025 = 9.75 %. (Not 10 %: adding double-counts the case where both fail.)
  2. Independent would need P(A and B) = 0.3 × 0.5 = 0.15; it's 0.1, so not independent (B makes A less likely). P(A | B) = 0.1 / 0.5 = 0.2.
  3. Per 10,000: 100 fraudsters → 80 flagged; 9,900 honest → 495 flagged. P(fraud | flag) = 80 / 575 = 13.9 %. Bayes: 0.8·0.01 / (0.8·0.01 + 0.05·0.99) = 0.008 / 0.0575 = 0.139.

A2

  1. E = 0.7·0 + 0.25·20 + 0.05·500 = 0 + 5 + 25 = €30. Most likely value: €0. And no — €30 never occurs; the expectation is an average, not an outcome. (This is the "mean of money" problem in miniature.)
  2. 3X: mean 30, σ 6 (variance scales by 9, σ by 3). X + 5: mean 15, σ unchanged at 2 (shifting doesn't change spread).
  3. Independent: 100 + 100 = 200. Perfectly dependent (same customer, both default together): the loss is 2X, so Var = 4 × 100 = 400. Correlation doubles the risk — that's concentration risk, and it's why binomial models understate portfolio variance when loans are correlated.
  4. z = (130 − 40) / 15 = 6. Yes, unusual — for a normal variable a 6σ event is one in a billion. But daily spend is heavy-tailed, so "unusual" is weaker than the normal rule suggests; still worth a look.
  5. Yes. Correlation only detects linear relations. Example: y = x² over a symmetric range: perfectly related, correlation 0. Or a U-shaped relation between age and default rate.

A3

  1. Binomial with n = 365, p = 0.003. Mean = 1.1 attempts. Because n is large and p small, Poisson with λ = 1.1 approximates it well: P(0) = e^(−1.1) ≈ 0.33.
  2. P(none) = 0.95¹⁰ = 0.599. P(exactly 1) = 10 · 0.05 · 0.95⁹ = 10 · 0.05 · 0.630 = 0.315. P(at least 2) = 1 − 0.599 − 0.315 = 0.086.
  3. λ for 5 minutes = 12 × 5/60 = 1. P(0) = e^(−1) = 0.368.
  4. €16 = 2σ. Beyond ±2σ is about 5 % → ~5 % of predictions off by more than €16.
  5. σ much bigger than the mean on a positive quantity means a long right tail — a few huge transactions. The mean is not a typical transaction. Ask for the median and the 90th/99th percentiles.
  6. Poisson would have variance ≈ 3. Variance 30 = ten times over-dispersed → not Poisson; disputes are clustering — a few merchants (or a few weeks) generate most of them. Look for a fraud ring, a broken merchant integration, or a data bug.
  7. Var = p(1 − p): at p = 0.5 it's 0.25, at p = 0.01 it's 0.0099. A 50/50 event is maximally uncertain — each observation carries the most information and you need the most data per unit of precision; a 1 % event is almost always "no", so each observation tells you little and rare "yes" cases are precious. That's why imbalanced classification is hard: the information is concentrated in the few positives.
