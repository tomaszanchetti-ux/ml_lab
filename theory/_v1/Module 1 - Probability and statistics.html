Probability & statistics for ML

A refresh, applied to the problems Revolut cares about — fraud, credit, disputes, A/B tests. Read once, then answer the questions at the end out loud, in English.

What this module is for

The “ML basis: maths + statistics” round. Not to make you a statistician — to make sure that when they say “the model is overconfident”, “the lift isn't significant” or “precision at that recall”, you know exactly what they mean and can say it back with a number.

Rule for the whole programme: every concept gets three things — the idea in plain words, the formula (so a symbol never scares you), and one Revolut-shaped example. Nothing else.

01Probability — the four rules you need

1 · Probability is a long-run frequency

P(A) = the fraction of times A happens if you repeat the situation many times. Always between 0 and 1; all outcomes together sum to 1.

RevolutIf 2 of every 1,000 card transactions are fraudulent, P(fraud) = 0.002. This number — the base rate — is the single most important number in any fraud or default problem, and the one people forget.

2 · Independence and the multiplication rule

Two events are independent if one tells you nothing about the other. Then P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B). If they are not independent: P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B | A).

Revolut“New device” and “fraud” are not independent — fraud is much more common on new devices. That dependence is exactly what a model learns. Treating dependent things as independent is the classic naive mistake (it's literally why “Naive Bayes” is called naive).

3 · Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

“Among the cases where B is true, how often is A true.” Every model output is a conditional probability: P(default | what we know about this customer).

4 · Bayes' theorem — updating a belief with evidence

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)

In words: what I believed before (P(A), the prior) × how well the evidence fits (P(B|A)), scaled by how common the evidence is overall (P(B)).

Revolut — the example to know by heart A fraud model flags a transaction. The model catches 90 % of fraud (P(flag | fraud) = 0.9) and wrongly flags 2 % of legitimate transactions (P(flag | legit) = 0.02). Fraud base rate 0.2 %. If a transaction is flagged, what's the probability it's actually fraud?
P(flag) = 0.9·0.002 + 0.02·0.998 = 0.0018 + 0.01996 = 0.02176
P(fraud | flag) = 0.9·0.002 / 0.02176 ≈ 0.083
Only ~8 %. A “90 % accurate” detector is wrong 92 % of the time it fires, because fraud is rare and the 2 % false-alarm rate on a huge legit population swamps it. This is why precision (next module) and the base rate matter more than “accuracy” in fraud, AML and credit. If you can explain this on a whiteboard, you've passed half the round.

02Random variables, expectation, variance

A random variable is a number whose value depends on chance: the amount of a transaction, whether a customer defaults (0/1), the number of disputes a merchant gets this month.

Expected value — the long-run average

E[X] = Σ xᵢ · P(xᵢ) (discrete) E[X] = ∫ x · p(x) dx (continuous)
RevolutExpected loss on a €1,000 loan with 4 % default probability and 60 % loss if default: E[loss] = 0.04 · 0.6 · 1000 = €24. That's the number the interest rate has to cover — exactly what your lender model was for: getting P(default) per customer so the price per customer is right.

Variance and standard deviation — how spread out

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] σ = √Var(X)

σ is in the same units as X, so it's the one you quote. Roughly: for many well-behaved variables, ~68 % of values fall within ±1σ of the mean, ~95 % within ±2σ.

RevolutA customer's typical spend is €40 ± €25 (mean ± σ). A €300 transaction is more than 10σ away — “unusual for this customer”. That z-score idea, z = (x − mean) / σ, is the simplest anomaly detector, and it's what PRAGMA does in a much richer way: “how far is this event from what this customer normally does”.

Covariance and correlation — do two things move together

corr(X, Y) = Cov(X, Y) / (σₓ σᵧ) between −1 and +1

Correlation ≠ causation — say it before they do. And correlation only measures linear relationships; two variables can be strongly related with correlation ≈ 0.

04Sampling, the Central Limit Theorem, confidence intervals

Why you can trust an average from a sample

Central Limit Theorem (CLT): take a sample of n values from almost any distribution and compute the mean. Do it many times: the means are normally distributed around the true mean, with standard deviation

standard error = σ / √n

Two consequences you must be able to say: (1) the sample mean is a good estimate of the true mean; (2) precision improves with √n, not n — to halve the error you need four times the data.

Confidence interval

95 % CI ≈ mean ± 1.96 · σ / √n
RevolutYou measure a 30 % dispute auto-resolution rate on 400 cases. For a proportion, σ = √(p(1−p)) = √(0.3·0.7) ≈ 0.46, so SE = 0.46 / √400 = 0.023, and the 95 % CI is 30 % ± 4.5 %. If someone asks “is it 30 or 32?”, the honest answer is “somewhere between 25 and 35 — we need more cases to say more.”

Bias vs. noise in sampling

More data fixes noise (random error). It does not fix bias (systematic error: your sample isn't like the population). Classic ML biases: survivorship (you only see loans you approved — you never learn what the rejected ones would have done), selection (the pilot ran on your best customers), label leakage (the training data contains the answer). Naming survivorship bias in a credit context is worth a lot in this round.

05Hypothesis tests, p-values, A/B tests

The logic

You want to know if a change had an effect. You assume it didn't (the null hypothesis, H₀) and ask: if H₀ were true, how surprising is the data I got? That surprise is the p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if there is no real effect. Small p (conventionally < 0.05) → reject H₀ → “statistically significant”.

What a p-value is NOT: it is not the probability that H₀ is true, and it is not the size of the effect. A tiny effect on a huge sample is “significant” and useless; a big effect on a small sample is “not significant” and maybe real. Always ask for the effect size and its confidence interval, not just the p.

The two errors

Reality: no effectReality: real effect
You say “effect”Type I error (false positive), probability α (usually 5 %)correct — “power” (usually target 80 %)
You say “no effect”correctType II error (false negative), probability β

Same table as a fraud model's false positives / false negatives — it's the same maths. Power is the chance you detect a real effect; it depends on effect size, sample size and α. Under-powered tests are the #1 A/B mistake.

A/B test, end to end

  1. Metric first (one primary, a few guardrails), and the minimum effect worth detecting (e.g. +1 pp on resolution rate).
  2. Sample size from that: roughly n per group ≈ 16 · σ² / δ² for 80 % power at α = 5 % (δ = the effect you want to detect). For a proportion, σ² = p(1−p).
  3. Randomise properly (by customer, not by session), run for full weekly cycles, don't peek and stop early — peeking inflates false positives.
  4. Read: effect size, CI, p. Check guardrails. Then decide.
RevolutBaseline auto-resolution 30 %; you want to detect a lift to 32 % (δ = 0.02). σ² = 0.3·0.7 = 0.21. n ≈ 16 · 0.21 / 0.0004 = 8,400 per group. If disputes run at 2,000/day, that's ~8–9 days for both arms. If someone proposes “let's test on 300 cases”, you can now say why that can't work.

Vocabulary to own

  • t-test: compares two means. Chi-square / z-test for proportions: compares two rates (most A/B tests). You don't need to compute them — know which is which.
  • Multiple comparisons: test 20 metrics at α = 5 % and one will be “significant” by luck. Pre-register the primary metric.
  • Novelty effect: a new feature spikes then fades. Run long enough.
  • Shadow mode (what you already use): the system decides, humans still decide, you compare — the safest first test for automations.

06One-page cheat sheet

  • Base rateHow common the thing is before any evidence. Fraud ≈ 0.1–0.5 %, default ≈ 2–10 %. Everything downstream depends on it.
  • Bayesposterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Rare events + imperfect detectors = most alerts are false. Hence precision.
  • E[X], σAverage and spread. Quote σ, not variance. z = (x − mean)/σ = “how unusual”.
  • Heavy tailsMoney is not normal. Medians and percentiles; log-transform.
  • CLTAverages are normal; error shrinks with √n. 4× data → 2× precision.
  • 95 % CImean ± 1.96 · σ/√n. Say the interval, not the point.
  • p-valueP(data this extreme | no effect). Not P(no effect). Effect size + CI > p.
  • PowerChance to detect a real effect; target 80 %. n ≈ 16σ²/δ² per group.
  • Bias vs. noiseMore data fixes noise, never bias. Survivorship bias in credit: you only see approved loans.
  • A/B hygieneOne primary metric, pre-set sample size, randomise by customer, full weeks, no peeking.

07Ten questions — answer out loud, in English, then check

  1. A model has 95 % accuracy on fraud detection. Why might that be useless? What would you ask for instead?
  2. Fraud base rate 0.5 %; detector catches 95 % of fraud and false-alarms on 1 % of legit. A transaction is flagged: probability it's fraud?
  3. Average customer balance is €2,300, median €410. What does that tell you, and which would you put on a dashboard?
  4. You have 100 data points and a standard error of 5. How many points do you need to get the standard error to 2.5?
  5. An A/B test shows p = 0.03 and a lift of 0.1 pp in conversion. Ship it?
  6. Your credit model is trained only on loans that were approved. What's the problem, and what's it called?
  7. Explain Type I and Type II error using a fraud alert as the example.
  8. Why shouldn't you stop an A/B test the first day it shows significance?
  9. Disputes per merchant per month: mean 4, variance 40. What might that suggest?
  10. What's the difference between P(flag | fraud) and P(fraud | flag), and which one does the business care about?

Answers (check after, not before)

  1. If fraud is 0.5 %, a model that says “never fraud” is 99.5 % accurate. Accuracy is meaningless with imbalanced classes. Ask for precision and recall (or PR-AUC) at the operating threshold, and the base rate.
  2. P(flag) = 0.95·0.005 + 0.01·0.995 = 0.00475 + 0.00995 = 0.0147. P(fraud | flag) = 0.00475 / 0.0147 ≈ 32 %. Two out of three alerts are false.
  3. Heavy right tail: a few very large balances drag the mean up; the typical customer has ~€410. Dashboard: median plus percentiles (p25/p75/p95); mean only for totals.
  4. Error shrinks with √n. Halving it needs 4× the data: 400 points.
  5. Probably not: significant but tiny. Ask: is 0.1 pp worth the cost and complexity? What's the CI — does it include zero? Were there multiple metrics tested? Effect size decides, not p.
  6. Survivorship / selection bias: you never observe what rejected applicants would have done, so the model learns from a biased population and can't evaluate its own rejections. Mitigations: reject inference, small random approval samples, external data.
  7. Type I = flagging a legitimate transaction as fraud (false positive; annoys a customer). Type II = missing a real fraud (false negative; loses money). The threshold trades one for the other.
  8. With repeated peeking the chance of a false “significant” somewhere along the way climbs far above 5 %. Fix the sample size in advance (or use sequential-testing methods designed for peeking).
  9. Variance ≫ mean means the counts are not Poisson — they're over-dispersed: disputes are clustered on some merchants or some periods. Look for a ring, a broken merchant, or a bug.
  10. P(flag | fraud) is recall (sensitivity): of real frauds, how many we catch. P(fraud | flag) is precision: of alerts, how many are real. Business cares about both, but ops teams live on precision (how many alerts are wasted) and losses depend on recall.
